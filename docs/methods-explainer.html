MechanicsGround Rules02 / 15

02You never generate a move

Unlike a normal Kaggle competition there is no submission.csv. You ship an agent bundle, and the engine hands your agent a menu.

What a submission is

  • deck.csv — 60 card IDs, one per line
  • main.py — exposes agent(obs) → [indices]
  • cg/ — the official engine, copied in from the competition input
  • All three packed into submission.tar.gz

So both the deck and the policy ship together. That single fact drives every result in this deck.

What a decision is

Every step the engine enumerates the legal options — ATTACH, PLAY, EVOLVE, ABILITY, RETREAT, ATTACK, END, plus sub-selections with minCount/maxCount.

Your agent returns an index into that menu. A turn is a sequence of picks ending in END, so you play several cards per turn.

The policy is pure menu-selection. It never invents an action; it only chooses among the ones already offered.

Why this mattersThe engine already sorts the menu sensibly. That gives you a brutally strong trivial baseline — "always pick option 0" — which most learned policies never beat. Hold that thought until slide 12.

Method Family 01Rule-Based Scoring06 / 15

06Score every option, take the max

The shared ancestor of the whole leaderboard. Both official samples describe the identical four-step loop, and every high scorer is a mutation of it.

1. obs_dict # receive board state 2. select.option # list the legal actions 3. score(each option) # hand-written card logic 4. return argmax # pick one index # Everything the scorer needs is visible: # opponent active HP + attached energy # both discard piles, all zone counts # your full hand, prizes remaining
Why it beats learning hereThe heuristic computes damage, KO reach and prize race from a fully visible board. It never has to recall or recognise a state — which is exactly what a 60-card hidden-information game makes impossible.

What the good ones encode

  • Named card constants, not generic types — 17 IDs in the Lucario pilot
  • Real ability math: Powerful Hand = 20 × hand size
  • Explicit KO / prize-count arithmetic per candidate attack
  • Opening-turn scripts (bench Abra + Dunsparce first, then two more Abra)

Cost of entry

These notebooks run 20,000–78,000 characters. The Alakazam and Lucario pilots are 700–800+ lines of card-specific logic. This is the real price of a top-ten score.

Method Family 02Meta Patching07 / 15

07The Crustle wall — and the patch

The highest-leverage public move of the early meta: noticing that one day-one deck hard-counters the entire ex-attacker field, and routing around it.

The problem

Crustle (card 345) has Mysterious Rock Inn: zero damage from Pokémon ex.

Mega Lucario ex is a mega-ex. A naive agent swings 270 damage into Crustle for 0, turn after turn, and throws a winnable matchup on autopilot.

The patch

The counter was already in the deck. Hariyama (674) is a non-ex 210-damage attacker.

Detect Crustle → stop feeding ex attacks into it → route energy and search toward the Makuhita→Hariyama line, backed by Makuhita/Hariyama 2→3, Switch 2→3, Poké Pad ×4.

Deck math, not vibes

Basics 10→12 (Riolu 3→4, Solrock 2→3) drops the mulligan rate 25.9% → 19.1%.

The list stays inside the Lucario shell on purpose: the policy is hardcoded to these cards, so a foreign netdeck would be piloted badly.

~10%→~70%
Crustle matchup win rate with the routing fix on, measured in-notebook.

How the wall aged

Crustle Wall ended at 7.75% of all classified decks — but only 3.7% of the 1100+ band and 11.8% down in 500–599. The counter-play propagated; the wall sank. Its lasting legacy is the crash-safety wrapper these notebooks popularised: every decision wrapped, legal minCount fallback, because validation plays you against a copy of yourself and a crash is a lost submission.

Method Family 03Forward Search08 / 15

08Search, bolted onto the heuristic

The engine exposes search_begin / search_step — you can genuinely simulate forward. Three notebooks used it, and all three kept the heuristic in charge.

BEAM_WIDTH = 3 # candidates kept per ply depth = 4 # plies, plus a time budget # candidates come FROM the heuristic, not from scratch base_order = AdvancedPolicy(obs).choose() for first in base_order[:MAX_CANDIDATES]: st = search_step(sid, [first]) beam.append((evaluate_state(st), ...)) # evaluate_state: hand-weighted, not learned prize_diff * 10000 energies attached * 200 Mega Lucario ex + 500 Hariyama + 300 own active HP * 2.0 opp active HP * -2.5 deck < 5 cards - 5000 # deck-out guard

The structure that scored

  • Layer 1 heuristic scores the legal options
  • Layer 2 search evaluates a small candidate set, if the API is available, within a per-decision time budget
  • Layer 3 legal fallback on any surprise

Two variants shipped

03 MultiPly Beam Search keeps a width-3 beam. 04 Probabilistic Expectimax adds simulate_action and averages over chance outcomes instead of taking the max. Same skeleton, same hand-weighted leaf value.

Result

900 and 915 — solid, but below the 1084.5 pure rule-based baseline. Rollouts inside the search are the same heuristic, capped at 20 steps. Search re-ranks the shortlist; it never replaces it.

09Modelling what you cannot see

The one genuinely new idea of the late meta, and the most-voted agent notebook of the whole competition. It attacks the hidden-information problem head-on instead of ignoring it.

def _op_visible(state, op_i): # what they've revealed def _match_archetype(op_seen): # which known deck is it? def _sample_hidden(state, me_i):# fill unseen zones def _leaf_eval(state, me_i): # score the completion def _greedy_pick(obs) # act on the belief
The honest constraintThe archetype template is static public reference data and is only consulted once the opponent reveals a matching line. It never inspects hidden opponent zones or replay identities — the belief is inferred, not peeked.

Why it matters to us

Our July conclusion was that beating the heuristic would need "a fundamentally different belief-state approach, not a static classifier — a research project."

Someone shipped exactly that. 213 votes, title claim LB 950+.

But read the scores honestly

The v21 Alakazam variant self-reports its exact score row at 798.3, with a campaign high-water of 844.4, and explicitly labels the earlier higher reads as volatility, not a stable claim.

Belief sampling is the best new idea in the field. It is not a demonstrated jump to 1300.

Method Family 05Learned & Optimised10 / 15

10Where learning actually appears

Across 6,808 teams and every public notebook, exactly one shipped agent contains a learned artefact — and it sits on top of a heuristic, not in place of one.

Tabular Q overlay — 812.2

The Iwapalace package: a self-contained heuristic plus a sanitised Q-value overlay in q_model_submit.json.

The real work is still hand-written resource guards: don't Hero's Cape anything but Iwapalace, don't over-attach Dark Energy, stop searching on a completed board.

Genetic algorithm on both halves

Deck counts and agent scoring weights are scalars, so a GA can optimise them jointly — each card given [id, init, min, max] bounds.

Published as a method write-up with no leaderboard claim. Matches our own GA result: +0.057 offline → −86 live.

Deep RL: attempted, never shipped

The official transformer + MCTS sample is the most-voted notebook in the competition (1,044 votes) and taught everyone the Search API.

A MuZero notebook appeared on 2026-08-20 as a 220-character stub. No published top entry runs deep RL.

PatternLearning survives only as an overlay or an offline optimiser on a working heuristic. Across the entire finished leaderboard, nobody shipped a learned policy as the decision-maker.

DiagnosisWhy Learning Lost13 / 15

13The 50.8% wall

The baseline nobody could beat

The engine already orders the option menu sensibly. Option 0 is the obvious play 50.8% of the time. Every classifier we trained — base features, rich features, more data — landed below that.

Where the expert's edge actually lives

In the remaining ~49% of deviations. Those depend on the hidden hand, an opponent read, and multi-turn planning. None of it is recoverable from a static, per-decision feature vector.

The field's answer was not a better classifier — it was belief sampling (slide 9): infer the hidden zones, then apply the same heuristic to the completed state.

Compute, don't recognise

The heuristic wins because everything it needs is visible and computable: opponent active HP and energy, both discard piles, every zone count.

It never needs to recall a similar state — which matters, because the hash-map policy showed held-out coverage of 0.3%.

The matched-deck gap

Top pilots on a shared deck hit 74–82%. Ours hit 38%. The decision mix is nearly identical (MAIN 61% vs 56%, draw 23% vs 27%) — so the ~40-point gap is choice quality inside the menu, plus an inconsistent go-first policy (we're 5/3; experts commit ~100% one way).

TakeawaysThe Playbook15 / 15

15What the finished board says to do

01 — Deck choice sets the ceiling

Mega Lucario is 36.6% of the bottom band and 3.7% of the top. Dragapult and Mega Kangaskhan are 59% of everything above 1100. Pick from the top band, then earn it with a matched pilot.

02 — Budget for the pilot, not the swap

~800 lines of card-specific logic per archetype. Our Grimmsnarl-on-a-generic-pilot experiment cost a live slot for 416.5.

03 — Crash-safety before cleverness

Wrap every decision with a legal minCount fallback. Validation plays you against yourself; one exception is a lost submission.

04 — Belief sampling is the live frontier

The one new idea that shipped and drew votes. Infer hidden zones from revealed cards, then let the heuristic act on the completed state. Search and clones both lost; this did not.

05 — Learning only as an overlay

A tabular Q file on a hand-written heuristic is the entire shipped ML footprint of a 6,808-team competition. Beat 50.8% offline before believing anything else.

06 — Where this goes next

This board closed 2026-08-16; we finished 3510 / 6808. The successor — PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy, $240,000, 483 teams — runs to 2026-09-13, and we are entered.
